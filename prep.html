01 Your pitch & positioning

Most people switching into GenAI over-apologize for "not being a researcher." Don't. Reframe the transition as an advantage.

The core narrative

Companies hiring for applied Gen AI roles are drowning in people who can call an LLM API and short on people who can turn that into a reliable, evaluated, cost-controlled production system. That second thing is engineering — your home turf.

30-second pitch template

"I've spent six years building production systems — [your domain: APIs, data pipelines, distributed services]. Over the last [X months] I've gone deep on LLM applications: I've built [RAG/agent project], learned to evaluate them rather than eyeball them, and I care about the parts most demos skip — latency, cost, failure modes, and grounding. I want to do that full-time: ship GenAI features that actually hold up in production."

Key mindset to signal

"An LLM is a probabilistic, unreliable component wrapped in a deterministic system I design." Interviewers love candidates who treat the model as one part of an engineered whole — not magic, not a black box to pray at.

02 What companies actually hire for

"Gen AI research" in a JD almost always maps to a handful of concrete business problems. Learn to recognize them — interviews are built around them.

1 · Knowledge assistants (RAG)

"Chat with our docs / support KB / contracts / codebase." The #1 enterprise use case. Problem they're solving: employees/customers can't find answers buried in scattered documents.

2 · Copilots & assistants

In-product AI that drafts, summarizes, or automates a workflow (email drafts, report generation, coding help). Problem: reduce time-per-task for the user.

3 · Agents / workflow automation

Multi-step automation: read a ticket → look up data → take an action. Problem: automate work that needs reasoning + tool use, not just a script.

4 · Extraction & classification

Turn unstructured text (invoices, resumes, tickets, reviews) into structured data. Problem: manual data entry / triage at scale.

5 · Search & recommendation

Semantic search, personalization, re-ranking with embeddings. Problem: keyword search misses intent.

6 · Content generation

Marketing copy, product descriptions, images. Problem: content bottleneck; personalization at scale.

The problems behind the problems

Every one of these breaks on the same failure modes. If you can speak to these, you sound senior:

  • Hallucination — the model invents facts. Mitigate with grounding (RAG), citations, "I don't know" behavior, evals.
  • Retrieval quality — RAG is only as good as what it retrieves. Chunking, embeddings, re-ranking, hybrid search.
  • Latency & cost — big models are slow and expensive. Caching, model routing, streaming, smaller models where possible.
  • Evaluation — "how do you know it works?" Offline evals, LLM-as-judge, human review, regression suites.
  • Safety — prompt injection, PII leakage, toxic output, jailbreaks. Guardrails, input/output filtering.
  • Non-determinism — same input, different output. Testing strategy, temperature control, structured outputs.

When an interviewer asks "how would you build X," structure your answer around these concerns. It's the difference between "I'd call GPT" and "here's the system."

03 Portfolio projects that get you hired

One strong, well-documented project beats three toy demos. It's your proof you can do the job — and your best interview material.

What "strong" means

  • Solves a real, specific problem (not "an AI chatbot").
  • Has evaluation — you measured quality, not just "it looks good."
  • Handles failure — what happens on a bad retrieval, an API error, a hallucination.
  • Has a writeup — a README or blog post explaining decisions and trade-offs. This is what interviewers actually read.

Three project ideas that map to real jobs

A · Grounded RAG assistant over a real corpus

Pick a domain you know (your company's public docs, a regulation, a codebase). Build ingestion → chunking → embeddings → retrieval → generation with citations. Add an eval set of ~30 Q&A pairs and measure retrieval hit-rate + answer faithfulness. Ship it with a simple UI.

Demonstrates: the #1 enterprise use case + evaluation discipline. Talk about chunking strategy, why you chose your embedding model, how you handle "not in the docs."

B · A small agent with real tools

An agent that takes a natural-language request and uses 2–3 tools (a search API, a calculator, a DB query) to complete a task. Handle tool errors, loop limits, and a "when to stop" policy.

Demonstrates: tool calling, orchestration, guardrails. Talk about your loop control, how you prevent infinite loops, structured tool outputs.

C · An eval harness

The most underrated project. Build a small framework that runs prompts against a dataset and scores outputs (exact match, LLM-as-judge, regex). Show a regression catch: "I changed the prompt and my eval caught a 12% quality drop."

Demonstrates: the exact engineering mindset that separates you from prompt-tinkerers. Senior interviewers love this.

Interview leverage

Rehearse a 3-minute walkthrough of your main project: the problem, your architecture, one hard trade-off you made, and how you knew it worked. Half of your technical interview can be steered onto ground you own.

04 Transformers & attention

You don't need to derive backprop. You need a clear mental model and to answer "how does attention work?" confidently.

The one-paragraph mental model

A transformer processes a sequence of tokens in parallel. Each token is a vector. Self-attention lets every token look at every other token and pull in relevant context, producing a new, context-aware vector for each position. Stack many attention + feed-forward layers, and you get a model that builds rich representations of language. LLMs are decoder-only transformers trained to predict the next token.

Attention, concretely

Each token produces three vectors: Query (Q), Key (K), Value (V). For a given token, you compare its Query to every token's Key (dot product) to get attention scores — "how much should I attend to each other token?" Softmax those scores into weights, then take a weighted sum of the Values. That's the token's new representation.

Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax( QKᵀ / √d_k ) · V

# QKᵀ      → similarity of each query to each key  (scores)
# / √d_k   → scale so softmax gradients stay stable
# softmax  → turn scores into weights that sum to 1
# · V      → weighted blend of value vectors

Terms you must be able to define

TermSay this
Multi-head attentionRun attention several times in parallel with different learned projections, so each "head" can focus on different relationships (syntax, coreference, etc.), then concatenate.
Positional encodingAttention is order-agnostic, so we inject position info. Modern models use rotary embeddings (RoPE) instead of the original sinusoidal ones.
Causal / masked attentionIn a decoder, a token can only attend to earlier tokens (mask the future) so it can't cheat at next-token prediction.
Feed-forward networkPer-token MLP after attention; where much of the model's "knowledge" capacity lives.
Residual + LayerNormSkip connections + normalization that make deep stacks trainable.
Context windowMax tokens the model can attend to at once. Attention is O(n²) in sequence length — the reason long context is expensive.
KV cacheAt inference, cache past Keys/Values so you don't recompute them each new token. Key to generation speed; consumes memory that grows with context.
Likely follow-up

"Why is attention O(n²)?" Because every token attends to every other token — n tokens × n comparisons. That's why 100k-token context is costly and why techniques like FlashAttention (IO-aware) and sparse/sliding-window attention exist.

05 How modern LLMs are trained

Know the pipeline stages and what each one does. This is a very common conceptual question.

StageWhat happensResult
1. PretrainingNext-token prediction over trillions of tokens of internet-scale text. Self-supervised — no labels needed.A "base model" with broad knowledge but no instruction-following.
2. SFT (supervised fine-tuning)Fine-tune on curated (instruction → good response) pairs.Model that follows instructions.
3. Preference tuningRLHF or DPO: align to human preferences using comparison data (response A better than B).Helpful, harmless, honest assistant behavior.

RLHF vs DPO — know the difference

  • RLHF (Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback): train a separate reward model on human preference data, then use RL (PPO) to optimize the LLM against that reward. Powerful but complex and unstable.
  • DPO (Direct Preference Optimization): skips the reward model and RL loop — directly optimizes the model on preference pairs with a simple classification-style loss. Simpler, more stable, now widely used.

Fine-tuning techniques (applied — you'll likely use these)

  • Full fine-tuning — update all weights. Expensive, rarely needed for app work.
  • LoRA / QLoRA — parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT). Freeze the base model, train small low-rank adapter matrices. QLoRA adds quantization so you can fine-tune large models on a single GPU. This is the practical answer to "how would you fine-tune?"
  • Quantization — store weights in lower precision (int8/int4) to shrink memory and speed inference, with small quality loss. Key for deployment.
Framing that impresses

"For most product problems I reach for prompting and RAG first, and only fine-tune when I need a consistent format/style/domain behavior that prompting can't reliably get — and then LoRA, not full fine-tuning." That's the pragmatic, senior answer.

06 Tokens, embeddings & decoding

The everyday mechanics you'll touch constantly. Expect quick-fire questions here.

Tokenization

Models don't see words — they see tokens (subword units via BPE / byte-pair encoding). "unbelievable" might be un+believ+able. Rules of thumb: ~4 chars/token in English, ~0.75 words/token. Why it matters: cost and context limits are counted in tokens; non-English and code tokenize less efficiently.

Embeddings

An embedding is a vector representation of text where semantic similarity ≈ geometric closeness. Used for search, RAG retrieval, clustering, classification. Compare with cosine similarity. This is a different model from the generative LLM (e.g. a dedicated embedding model).

Decoding / sampling parameters

ParamWhat it doesWhen to change it
temperatureRandomness. 0 = deterministic/greedy, higher = more diverse/creative.Low (0–0.3) for extraction/classification; higher (0.7–1.0) for brainstorming.
top_p (nucleus)Sample only from the smallest set of tokens whose probs sum to p.Alternative to temperature; usually tune one, not both.
max_tokensCap on output length.Control cost/latency; prevent runaway generation.
stop sequencesStrings that end generation.Structured outputs, delimiters.
frequency/presence penaltyDiscourage repetition / encourage new topics.Reduce loops and repetition.
Common trap

"Temperature 0" is not fully deterministic in practice (floating point, MoE routing, batching). If asked how to make outputs reliable, the answer is structured outputs + evals + low temperature, not "just set temp to 0."

07 RAG deep-dive

If you learn one system cold, make it this. RAG is the most common thing you'll be asked to design and build.

Why RAG exists

LLMs have a fixed knowledge cutoff, don't know your private data, and hallucinate. Retrieval-Augmented Generation fixes this: fetch relevant documents at query time and put them in the prompt as context, so the model answers grounded in real sources instead of its memory.

The two pipelines

INGESTION (offline)
  documents → clean/parse → chunk → embed → store in vector DB

QUERY (online)
  user query → embed → similarity search (top-k chunks)
             → [optional re-rank] → build prompt with context
             → LLM generates grounded answer + citations

Where RAG goes wrong (and your fixes)

FailureCauseFix
Retrieves irrelevant chunksBad chunking / weak embeddingsTune chunk size & overlap; better embedding model; add a re-ranker
Right doc, wrong chunkChunk too small, context splitLarger chunks, overlap, or retrieve neighboring chunks
Misses keyword/exact matchesPure semantic searchHybrid search: combine dense (vector) + sparse (BM25/keyword)
Answers from memory, ignores contextWeak prompt / conflicting knowledgeInstruct "answer only from context; say you don't know otherwise"
Hallucinated citationsNo verificationReturn source chunk IDs; validate citations against retrieved set
Too much/irrelevant contextTop-k too highRe-rank and trim; "lost in the middle" — models under-attend to mid-context

Key knobs to discuss

  • Chunking — size (e.g. 256–1024 tokens), overlap, and strategy (fixed, recursive, semantic, or structure-aware by heading/section). This choice makes or breaks retrieval.
  • Embedding model — dimension, domain fit, cost, multilingual needs.
  • Vector store — pgvector, Pinecone, Weaviate, Qdrant, FAISS. Index type (HNSW) trades recall vs speed.
  • Re-ranking — retrieve top-50 cheaply, then a cross-encoder re-ranks to top-5 for precision.
  • Hybrid search — dense + sparse fused (e.g. reciprocal rank fusion). Big real-world win.
  • Evaluation — retrieval metrics (hit-rate, MRR, recall@k) separate from generation metrics (faithfulness, relevance).
Say this to sound senior

"I evaluate retrieval and generation separately — most RAG bugs are retrieval bugs, and if you only look at the final answer you can't tell whether the model or the retriever failed."

08 Agents & tool use

The current frontier of applied work. Know what an agent is, and — importantly — when not to build one.

Definitions

  • Tool / function calling — the model outputs a structured request to call a function (name + JSON args); your code runs it and feeds the result back. The foundation of everything agentic.
  • Agent — an LLM in a loop that decides which tools to call, observes results, and repeats until a goal is met. "Model + tools + loop + stopping condition."
  • ReAct — Reason + Act: the model alternates reasoning ("thought") and tool calls ("action") with observations in between.
  • Orchestration — multi-step / multi-agent flows: routers, planners, workers, supervisors.

The hard parts (this is what interviewers probe)

  • Stopping conditions — max steps, confidence checks, explicit "done" tool. Prevent infinite loops and runaway cost.
  • Error handling — tools fail. Retry, fall back, or surface to the user. Never let one bad tool call crash the loop silently.
  • Context growth — every step adds to the prompt. Summarize, prune, or use scratchpads.
  • Reliability — agents compound errors: 90% per-step accuracy over 5 steps ≈ 59% success. This is why agents are hard.
  • Cost/latency — each step is an LLM call. Multi-step agents are slow and expensive.
The mature take

"Prefer the simplest thing that works: a single well-prompted call, then a fixed chain/workflow, and only a full autonomous agent when the task genuinely needs dynamic decision-making. Agents add reliability and cost problems — don't reach for one to look sophisticated." Interviewers reward restraint here.

MCP (Model Context Protocol) — an open standard for connecting models to tools/data sources through a common interface, so you don't hand-wire every integration. Worth knowing by name; increasingly common in JDs.

09 Prompting vs RAG vs fine-tuning

A classic decision question: "the model doesn't do X well — what do you do?" Have a crisp framework.

Use…When the problem is…Example
Prompt engineeringBehavior/format — the model can do it, just needs guidanceBetter instructions, few-shot examples, clearer output schema
RAGKnowledge — it lacks facts (private, fresh, or specific data)Answer questions about your company's docs
Fine-tuningConsistent behavior/style/format at scale, or a narrow domain skill prompting can't reliably hitAlways output your exact JSON schema; match a brand voice; classify domain jargon
The rule of thumb to quote

"Fine-tuning changes behavior; RAG changes knowledge. If the model doesn't know something, don't fine-tune — retrieve. If it knows but won't act the way you need consistently, fine-tuning helps. And always try prompting first — it's the cheapest iteration loop."

Prompt-engineering techniques to name-drop correctly

  • Zero-shot / few-shot — no examples vs a few in-context examples.
  • Chain-of-thought — "think step by step" for reasoning tasks (less needed with reasoning models).
  • Structured output — enforce JSON via schema / tool calling for reliable parsing.
  • System prompt — set role, constraints, and guardrails separate from user input.
  • Prompt as code — version it, test it, review it. It is part of your codebase.

10 Evaluation & guardrails

This is the single most senior-signaling topic and the one prompt-tinkerers can't speak to. Invest here.

Why evals matter

LLM outputs are non-deterministic and open-ended — you can't unit-test them like normal code. Without evals you're flying blind: you can't tell if a prompt change, model upgrade, or new feature made things better or worse. "How do you evaluate?" is a near-guaranteed interview question.

Types of evaluation

MethodHowGood for
Deterministic checksExact match, regex, JSON-valid, contains-keywordExtraction, classification, format compliance
Reference-basedCompare to gold answer (semantic similarity, BLEU/ROUGE)Tasks with known answers
LLM-as-judgeA model scores outputs on a rubric (faithfulness, relevance, tone)Open-ended answers, chat quality, RAG faithfulness
Human reviewAnnotators rate outputsGround truth, calibrating your LLM judge
A/B / onlineReal users, real metrics (thumbs, task completion)Production truth

RAG-specific metrics (RAGAS-style)

  • Faithfulness — is the answer supported by the retrieved context? (catches hallucination)
  • Answer relevance — does it actually address the question?
  • Context precision/recall — did retrieval fetch the right chunks?
Watch-outs on LLM-as-judge

Name the pitfalls: judges are biased (prefer longer answers, their own outputs, position bias), so calibrate against human labels, use clear rubrics, and don't trust a judge you haven't spot-checked.

Guardrails / safety

  • Prompt injection — malicious input overrides your instructions ("ignore previous instructions…"). Mitigate: separate system/user roles, don't blindly trust retrieved/tool content, output validation, least-privilege tools.
  • PII / data leakage — redact inputs, restrict what the model can access and output.
  • Content safety — moderation filters on input and output.
  • Jailbreaks — attempts to bypass safety. Defense in depth, not a single filter.

11 System design (GenAI edition)

Your SWE experience shines here. Same discipline as classic system design — new components.

A framework for any "design an LLM system" question

  1. Clarify — users, scale (QPS), latency budget, cost ceiling, data sources, quality bar, offline vs real-time.
  2. Data flow — ingestion, storage, retrieval, the LLM call, post-processing, response.
  3. The LLM layer — which model(s), prompt design, structured output, streaming.
  4. Reliability — timeouts, retries, fallbacks (cheaper model / cached / graceful degradation), rate limits.
  5. Quality — evals, guardrails, monitoring, human-in-the-loop where stakes are high.
  6. Cost & latency — caching (semantic cache), model routing (small model for easy queries), batching, token trimming.
  7. Observability — logging/tracing prompts & responses, cost dashboards, quality regression alerts.

Worked example: "Design a customer-support AI assistant"

Clarify: answers from help-center + past tickets; must cite sources; escalate to humans; ~10 QPS peak; <3s response; must not hallucinate policy.

Architecture: RAG over help-center (ingestion pipeline → chunk → embed → vector DB w/ hybrid search + re-ranker). Query → retrieve → grounded generation with citations → guardrail check → stream to user.

Reliability: if retrieval confidence is low or the model is unsure → "I'm not certain, connecting you to an agent." Cache common questions (semantic cache). Fallback model on timeout.

Quality: offline eval set of real tickets (faithfulness + resolution). Log thumbs up/down. Weekly regression run on prompt/model changes. PII redaction, prompt-injection filter on retrieved content.

Cost: route simple FAQs to a small/cheap model; big model only for complex queries. Trim context via re-ranking. Track cost-per-conversation.

What they're grading

Not whether you know a fancy trick — whether you treat the LLM as one unreliable component in a system you make reliable. Talk trade-offs, failure modes, and how you'd measure success. That's your SWE edge; use it.

12 Coding problems

Two flavors: (a) normal DSA/LeetCode still shows up, and (b) GenAI-specific implementation. Below are the GenAI ones you should be able to write from scratch.

Cosine similarity + top-k retrieval

import numpy as np

def cosine(a, b):
    a, b = np.asarray(a), np.asarray(b)
    return a @ b / (np.linalg.norm(a) * np.linalg.norm(b) + 1e-9)

def top_k(query_vec, doc_vecs, docs, k=3):
    scored = [(cosine(query_vec, dv), d) for dv, d in zip(doc_vecs, docs)]
    scored.sort(key=lambda x: x[0], reverse=True)
    return scored[:k]

Text chunking with overlap

def chunk(text, size=500, overlap=50):
    words, chunks, i = text.split(), [], 0
    while i < len(words):
        chunks.append(" ".join(words[i:i + size]))
        i += size - overlap          # step back by overlap
    return chunks

Robust LLM call: retry with backoff + timeout

import time

def call_with_retry(fn, *, retries=3, base=1.0):
    for attempt in range(retries):
        try:
            return fn()
        except (RateLimitError, TimeoutError) as e:
            if attempt == retries - 1:
                raise
            time.sleep(base * (2 ** attempt))   # exponential backoff
    # add jitter in production to avoid thundering herd

A minimal agent loop

def run_agent(query, tools, llm, max_steps=5):
    context = [f"User: {query}"]
    for step in range(max_steps):          # bounded — no infinite loops
        action = llm(context, tools)       # model picks a tool or answers
        if action.type == "final":
            return action.answer
        try:
            result = tools[action.name](**action.args)
        except Exception as e:
            result = f"ERROR: {e}"          # feed errors back, don't crash
        context.append(f"{action.name} -> {result}")
    return "Stopped: step limit reached."   # explicit stopping condition

Tiny LLM-as-judge eval loop

def evaluate(dataset, system, judge):
    passed = 0
    for row in dataset:
        out = system(row["input"])
        verdict = judge(question=row["input"], answer=out,
                        reference=row.get("expected"))
        passed += verdict["score"] >= 0.7
    return passed / len(dataset)            # your quality metric

13 Question bank

Click each to reveal a strong answer. Rehearse out loud — knowing ≠ saying it cleanly under pressure.

What's the difference between fine-tuning and RAG, and when do you pick each?

RAG adds knowledge at query time by retrieving relevant documents into the prompt — best when the model lacks facts (private, fresh, or specific data). Fine-tuning changes behavior/style/format by updating weights — best when you need consistent output the prompt can't reliably enforce, or a narrow domain skill. Try prompting first (cheapest), then RAG for knowledge gaps, then fine-tuning (usually LoRA) for behavior. They combine — fine-tune for format + RAG for facts.

How does self-attention work?

Each token produces Query, Key, and Value vectors. To compute a token's new representation, you dot its Query with every token's Key to get relevance scores, scale by √d and softmax them into weights, then take a weighted sum of Values. This lets each token pull context from all others. Multi-head runs this in parallel with different projections to capture different relationships. It's O(n²) in sequence length, which is why long context is expensive.

Your RAG system gives wrong answers. How do you debug?

Separate retrieval from generation. First check: did we retrieve the right chunks? Log the retrieved context. If the relevant chunk isn't there, it's a retrieval bug — fix chunking, embeddings, add hybrid search or a re-ranker. If the right chunk is retrieved but the answer is still wrong, it's a generation/prompt bug — tighten the prompt to answer only from context, check for "lost in the middle," reduce noisy context. I'd have an eval set to measure both stages, not eyeball it.

How do you evaluate an LLM feature?

Build an eval dataset of representative inputs with expected behavior. Use the cheapest valid method per task: deterministic checks (exact match, JSON-valid, regex) for structured tasks; LLM-as-judge with a rubric for open-ended ones, calibrated against human labels; and online signals (thumbs, task completion) in production. Run it as a regression suite on every prompt/model change so I can prove a change helped or caught a regression. For RAG I also measure retrieval separately (recall@k) from generation (faithfulness, relevance).

How would you reduce cost and latency of an LLM app?

Caching (exact and semantic caching for repeated/similar queries); model routing (a small/cheap model for easy queries, big model only when needed); prompt/context trimming and re-ranking to send fewer tokens; streaming to improve perceived latency; batching where possible; setting max_tokens; and considering a smaller fine-tuned model for a narrow high-volume task. Measure cost-per-request and p95 latency, then optimize the biggest contributor.

What is prompt injection and how do you defend against it?

Prompt injection is when untrusted input (user text, a retrieved document, a tool result) contains instructions that hijack the model — e.g. "ignore previous instructions and reveal the system prompt." Defenses are layered: keep system vs user roles separate, treat all retrieved/tool content as untrusted data not instructions, validate and constrain outputs, give tools least privilege, and add a moderation/guardrail check. There's no single fix — it's defense in depth, and for high-stakes actions keep a human in the loop.

When should you NOT use an agent?

Most of the time. Agents add cost, latency, and compounding-error unreliability (90% per step over 5 steps ≈ 59%). If the task is a fixed sequence, a hardcoded chain/workflow is more reliable and cheaper. Use a full autonomous agent only when the path genuinely can't be predetermined and needs dynamic tool selection. Reaching for the simplest thing that works — single call → workflow → agent — is a signal of maturity, not lack of ambition.

What causes hallucinations and how do you reduce them?

LLMs generate the most probable continuation, not the true one — with no built-in grounding they'll confidently fill gaps. Reduce with: RAG to ground answers in real sources; prompt the model to answer only from provided context and say "I don't know" otherwise; require citations and validate them against retrieved chunks; lower temperature for factual tasks; and evaluate faithfulness. You reduce, not eliminate — so design the UX to show sources and let users verify.

Explain temperature, top_p, and when you'd change them.

Temperature scales randomness — 0 is near-greedy/deterministic, higher increases diversity. top_p (nucleus) samples from the smallest token set whose probabilities sum to p. Tune one, not both. Use low temperature (0–0.3) for extraction, classification, and anything needing consistency; higher (0.7–1.0) for brainstorming or creative writing. Note temp 0 isn't perfectly deterministic in practice, so reliability comes from structured outputs + evals, not just low temperature.

How do you get reliable structured (JSON) output?

Use the provider's structured-output / tool-calling mode with a schema rather than hoping free-text parses. Provide a clear schema and a few examples, set low temperature, and validate the result (schema check) with a retry/repair step on failure. Never regex-scrape JSON out of prose in production — constrain generation to the schema.

Why is your SWE background valuable for this role?

Because the model is the easy part — turning it into a reliable product is engineering. I bring production instincts: observability, testing, cost/latency tuning, failure handling, and data pipelines. GenAI systems live or die on evals, retrieval quality, guardrails, and monitoring — all engineering disciplines. I treat the LLM as one probabilistic component in a system I design to be reliable, which is exactly what shipping GenAI features needs.

What's the context window, and what problems come with long context?

It's the max number of tokens the model can attend to at once (prompt + output). Because attention is O(n²), long context is slow and expensive, and models exhibit "lost in the middle" — they attend less to information buried mid-context. So more context isn't free or always better; retrieving and re-ranking the right few chunks usually beats stuffing everything in.

  • "How do you keep up with a fast-moving field?" — name real habits: a paper or newsletter you follow, things you've built, how you evaluate hype.
  • "Disagreed with a teammate." — data-driven resolution.
The switch story — structure

Trigger ("Building X, I kept hitting problems that were really about ML/LLMs…") → Action ("So I built [project], learned evals/RAG, studied the fundamentals") → Fit ("My engineering background is exactly what makes GenAI features reliable") → Intent ("I want to do this full-time, and this role is the right place"). Rehearse it to ~60 seconds.

  • "How much of the role is research vs shipping to production?"
